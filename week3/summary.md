Coronary heart disease (CHD) was the leading cause of US death at the time this trial came out. Other trials had been done but there was nothing that had proven the causality and the other trials happened to have been inconclusive. The trial presented here tested whether lowering LDL-C could also lower CHD using the drug cholestyramine. The design of the trial was a double blind placebo controlled trial that ran for 7-10 years. The participants were only males age 35-59 who had primary hypercholesterolemia (type II hyperlipoproteinemia). There were many people excluded from the trial due to sex, other confounders that were similar to other heart diseases, and responses to diet. After the exclusions, 3,806 men were chosen and randomized into the two groups (placebo and treatment). The ones who were placed into the treatment group got 6 packets of cholestyramine per day (lowered if needed); the placebo group got placebo packets. Both groups also were on cholesterol lowering diets, had to go to bimonthly clinic visits, and had their diet measured. The diet alone was able to lower the total amount of cholesterol in both of the groups. There was additional falls of LDL-C on cholestyramine; it was 45.9 mg/dL for the treatment and 5.7 for the control. The two groups also both seemed to have GI side effects; though they weren't severe. Overall lowering LDL-C helped reduce CHD with men who were hypercholesterolemic.

When I was reading this trial, what interested me most was the covariates and confounders. I'm curious how they decided which factors were enough to leave someone out of the study. However, even after leaving all those people out, controlling something like diet brings in its own confounders, since they can't actually watch what anyone eats. The deaths do this too. It's interesting to read how no matter how much you try to control, life is hard to control, especially with a trial this long.

What didn't suprise me was the exclusion of women or children. What did surprise me was the claim that the results extend to women and other age groups. They themselves excluded such a large group of screened men and women to protect the trial from confounding. Extending the findings to populations they deliberately kept out does not follow the data.


Discussion questions: 

1) A number of men in this trial died from things unrelated to heart disease, and more of them were in the drug group. The researchers seemed sure the drug wasn't the reason why. Was that a fair call they could make?

2) The men who took this drug for 7-10 years did it without knowing whether they were on the real thing. Is that a fair thing to ask of people then? What about now?
