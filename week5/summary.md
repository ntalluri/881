This commentary piece reviews and questions screening mammography for women starting at age 40. He focused on eight randomized controlled trials that had breast cancer mortality as the endpoint. Using the Mantel–Haenszel method assumed all the trials share a relative risk and it estimated an 18% mortality reduction (with a 95% CI from 5% to 29%); however, Berry argued that the trials are very heterogeneous (the trials differ in population, screening interval, mammography quality, control conditions, and analysis type) which makes this type of analysis incorrect. He argued that a different model that allowed for the heterogeneity without assuming it should be used (such as a random effects model). He applied a hierarchical Bayesian random-effects model where the estimate still stayed around 18% (with a 95% CI from −1% to 36%). Assuming the 18% persists, he mentioned that screening can add about 5 days of life expentency per woman. 

Berry also defends inclusing the Canadian trial, which happened to be the largest trial and the only one designed for this age group; giving the control group clinical breast examinations is a legitimate comparison of two screening strategies, not a reason for exclusion. He also argued that post-hoc power calculations are irrelevant and that the Gothenburg trial is just as equal an outlier. Kopans and Halpern replied that the Canadian trial was underpowered, used unblinded randomization, and had poor-quality mammography. They also argued that Berry dismissed statistical significance. Berry responded that significance shows whether a benefit exists, not whether it matters to an individual woman. He added that assuming homogeneity is a separate issue from Bayesian versus frequentist methods.

Berry concludes that screening in the forties probably lengthens some women's lives, but the benefit is very small. Any benefit falls on the 1 in 60 - 70 women diagnosed with breast cancer in their forties, so Berry compares screening to winning the lottery. He compares the 5 days lost by delaying mammography for 10 years to familiar risks such as not wearing a seat belt over 20 years of typical driving, cycling for 15 hours without a helmet, or gaining two ounces of body weight. Berry finds no trial evidence that annual screening outperforms screening every other year, while annual screening roughly doubles the risks. He recommends that every woman should weigh the benefits and risks and decide for themselves.

I have always wondered why I need to wait until 40 to start getting screened for breast cancer. I assumed I did not need to start sooner since my family does not seem to be at risk, and since America is more of a treatment country than a prevention country. I also assumed insurance would never cover early screening by choice, and that there would need to be evidence showing when screening should start. After reading Berry, the age cutoff makes more sense to me. Breast cancer is less common in younger women, so even if screening worked equally well at every age, fewer younger women would truly benefit from it.

Being told that every woman should weigh the benefits and risks and decide for herself just makes me feel like "what in the world should I do?". Berry does explain that many people struggle to understand risk when it is given as numbers, yet the recommendation still leaves the decision to the patient. The risks of anxiety and pain sounded like weird excuses to me. Some worry or discomfort seems minor next to catching cancer early. The false positive argument is different; that is a real cost, and it makes a valid point against over screening.
