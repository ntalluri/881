The main argument for this paper focuses on the statistical community focusing on a data modeling culture (assumes a stochastic data model for the inside of the black box to explain y from input x.) prevented statisticians in doing better modeling. By doing better, this means that the data modeling approach led to irrelevant theory, questionable concludions,  prevention of new problems, and not using more suitable algorithmic models. The algorithmic model culture considers the inside of the box complex and unknown with their approach being just to find some function fx that operates as an algorithm to predict y from input x. From this papers argument, the author fights for the algorithmic models tend to be better ways of predicting when it comes to most applications. 

When approaching any problem thouhg, the best solution could be both a data or algorithmic model. The data itself and the goal of the problem being solved leads to what the solution can be. However, sticking to just a data model restricts a statisticaions ability to solve the problem when in realtity a much wider set of approaches are needed to find a solution.

At the time of this article the methods for algorithmic modeling were neural networks, decision trees, and support vector machines. The approach focused on the fact aht nature produces data that in itself is a black box and instead we want to find some function that can be that black box when looking at prediciting a set of y's when given a set of input x's. 

The author seems to really love decision trees, and a lot of his own work comes from them. In his chlorine project, a decision tree got 95% accuracy on predicting whether a compound contained chlorine from its mass spectra, which was something linear and quadratic discriminant analysis struggled with. He also gives single decision trees an A+ on interpretability but only a B on prediction. To improve prediction, he grows a forest of trees and has them vote, which lowered the test error on every data set he compared. The catch is that once combined, around 100 trees together, we lose that interpretability, and he gives random forests an F on it. He seems okay with this tradeoff though, since he argues the goal should be accurate information and not interpretability.

The author addresses the tradeoff between accuracy and interpretability problem. He points out that nature itseld is already a black box, and the most accurate prediction methods are also black boxes, so we end up using one black box to understand another. He mentions that his biostatistician friends say doctors can interpret logistic regression but not fifty trees hooked together, so when given the choice they will pick interpretability. However, the author argues this is the wrong question to ask. The point of a model is to get useful information about how the inputs relate to the output, and interpretability is just one way of getting that information. A model does not have to be simple or a data model to give reliable information, so the goal should be accurate information and not interpretability.

When the author was listing their perceptions on statistical analysis, this part "live with the data before you plunge into
modeling" stuck with me. I feel like I see a lot of papers now / machine learning culture that seems to really ignore what their data is and instead focuses more on the machine learning aspect. It's just really weird to me that being forget about trash data going in leads to trash output. With that, without understanding their data fully leads to unrealistic conclusions.

It's so interesting to have to think about this problem from the my own personal experience. When working with biologists, when we are trying to understand some mechanistic problem, they usually do want some way to explain how the model is working and less on just a black box give back and answer. So while I understand this author's point that a better model would be something that is more black boxed, in some cases we need to use simpler or well understood models to be able to explain the reasonings for the answer. I also think this helps when it comes to having to validate something in the lab. Validation isn't something quick, it can take years, so maybe with the reasoning being explained, validation isn't wasted on something that was black boxed away and we just have to hope it is correct. In my work though, I feel like a mix of both, a simpler data modeling approach helps in us understanding the data (such as logistic or linear regression to be able to produces weights that give a level of importance of each of the variables/ inputs) but once we can see some conclusions we can move to use more algorithmic modeling approaches to help make better predictions. 

People care about interpribility of their models now. It is really hard to do most of the time, but it is getting to a point where people want more complex models but do want to understand the black box.
