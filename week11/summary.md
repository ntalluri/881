Benjamini and Hochberg define the false discovery rate (FDR) in this paper. Controlling the familywise error rate (FWER), the probability of making even one false positive, costs too much power to detect real effects. Demanding zero false positives is more than most need: if we make 100 discoveries and 5 are wrong, the overall conclusion still holds. They propose controlling the FDR instead, the expected proportion of rejected null hypotheses (the discoveries) that are false positives. Formally, with V = false positives and R = total rejections, FDR = E(V/R). Their procedure sorts the p-values in ascending order, then finds the largest index i satisfying P(i) ≤ (i/m)q*, where q* is the chosen FDR level (like 0.05). All hypotheses with p-values at or below that cutoff are rejected.

The paper tested the FDR procedure on a trial where researchers compared two clot-dissolving drugs across 15 outcomes and claimed the new drug lowered mortality, using p = 0.0095 with no correction at all. The FWER methods set a cutoff near 0.003 or 0.004 and rejected only the three smallest p-values, so the mortality claim failed. The FDR procedure scales its cutoff by rank, giving mortality a threshold of 0.0133, which it clears, so the mortality finding survives with an expected false share of at most 5%. This example shows that FWER control was not only conservative but it also discarded a result the data supported.

When I was reading this paper, it hit me how much of an impact FDR had on analysis. The authors were not proposing a sharper test, rather they were responding to the fact that researchers had stopped correcting for multiplicity at all. Their fix was to change what gets controlled, so the correction stays strict when there is nothing to find and loosens when real effects are actually present.
