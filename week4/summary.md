This combination of papers was based around the Nurses’ Health Study (NHS) observational trial. The original findings of this trial found that hormones lowered heart disease risk; however, the Women’s Health Initiative (WHS) randomized trial said hormones raised heart disease risk. These two trials disagreed. Instead of letting the differences attribute to the lack of randomization in the observational trial, the NHS trial decided figure out if there was other reasons for this disagreement. They redid their analysis on their data to mimic the WHS analysis. They used the same eligibility rules set by WHI. For the NHS study, they had 8 pooled "trials"; they had a survey every 2 years where they compared women who started hormones with women who did not. They also ran two versions of the trials. They has an intention-to-treat (ITT) version (women stay in their starting group, even if they switch later) and adherence-adjusted (estimates the effect if no one switched). They had 34575 women eligible from this style of trials. 

For the ITT results, they found that women who started the hormone therapy had a higher risk of CHD early in follow-up. The HR was 1.83 (1.05 to 3.17) in the first year and 1.42 (0.92 to 2.20) in the first 2 years. Over the entire follow-up, the HR was 0.96 (0.78 to 1.18), indicating no clear difference in risk. When stratified by time since menopause, the HR was 0.84 (0.61 to 1.14) in women who started therapy within 10 years of menopause and 1.12 (0.84 to 1.48) in women who started 10 or more years after. For the adherence-adjusted results, followed a similar pattern. The HR was 1.61 (0.97 to 2.66) in the first 2 years and 0.98 (0.66 to 1.49) over the entire follow-up. The difference by time since menopause was much larger: the HR was 0.54 (0.19 to 1.51) in women within 10 years of menopause and 1.20 (0.78 to 1.84) in women 10 or more years after. When the NHS data was analyzed to match the design of the WHI trial, the estimates actually became similar to the WHI estimates, so the authors concluded that differences in time since menopause and length of follow-up explain most of the differences between the two studies. They also concluded that there could be residual confounding in the NHS trial. These results support a timing hypothesis, which states that hormone therapy may increase CHD risk mainly in women who start it 10 or more years after menopause.

The trial results sparked controversy and debate among doctors, researchers, the media, and the people who were affected by these results (menopausal women). Robert Hoover argued that the debate actual led to better methods for comparing and combining study types, and he did agree that that both studies did show increased CHD risk shortly after starting hormones, especially in women who start more than 10 years after menopause. He also notes that trial results may not apply to everyone, that trials rarely have enough participants to test subgroups, and that including older women in the WHI showed that timing matters. Meir Stampfer agrees that timing explains most of the difference between the studies and that hidden confounding is unlikely, but he argues that applying ITT to observational data combines the weaknesses of the design (no randomization and when women switch treatment). He finds the method used was too complex and hard to follow and argues that it adds no new insights so he recommends for the procedure to not be used. Ross Prentice did praise the author's careful matching of NHS women to the WHI design and notes that starting follow-up at hormone initiation corrects the bias caused by the NHS's 2-year surveys; however, he does warn that only a true randomized trial guarantees comparable groups. Even the authors of the main paper disagreed about the value of their method. Hernán and Robins argued that their approach does resolve the NHS and WHI disagreement and shows that biases in the NHS are likely small, and exposes that the NHS method is the real black box method; however, coauthors Willett, Manson, and Grodstein side with Stampfer and do not recommend the method for use, arguing that copying the WHI design gave too much weight to women who started hormones long after menopause.
