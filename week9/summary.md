The paper evaluates the parametric statistical methods used in three different fMRI software packages (SPM, FSL, AFNI). These methods each had been validated mainly on simulated data, which does not reproduce the noise of real scans. So, the main question focuses in this paper focuses on seeing if the parametric statistics in each of the fMRI software control's false positives at a 5% nominal rate. They explore to see why each of the methods fail to control the false positive risk. 

The authors used resting state fMRI data from  499 healthy controls; the subjects perfromed no task so any that were detected as activation was marked as a false positive. They analyzed the data as if they came from a task experiement and analyzed the data 1000 times across 192 combinations of site, task design, smoothing level, group size, and test type. Each was run through each of the fMRI software packages and a nonparametric permutation test. 

They found that the voxel wise inferecence (a statistical and computational approach in neuroimaging used to analyze brain data at the resolution of individual voxels (3D pixels) across the brain volume or surface) was valid in all three of the packages, with familywise error rates below the nominal 5%. However, they saw that the cluster wise inference (a two-step statistical procedure most commonly used to correct for multiple comparisons in functional magnetic resonance imaging) was invalid, reaching around 70% familywise error rates. They saw that the clustering threshold was driving  most of the differences. The cluster define threshold (CDT) P value (P) = 0.001 performed better than P = 0.01, and the common rule of P = 0.001 with a 10-voxel extent threshold produced error rates of 60–90%. The permuation test they did held the nominal 5% error rate for two-sample tests, but varied for any one-sample tests because it assumed the data are symmetric, where it it ranged 0.8% to 40% error. 

The parametric methods rely on random field theory, which models that nearby voxels in the brain are correlated and assumes that the data is equally smooth everywhere in the brain. However this is not true for real fMRI data. In real fMRI data, voxels stay correlated over longer distances than the model expects, so clusters grow bigger than it predicts, and ordinary-sized clusters get mistaken for real activation which creates more false positive clusters.  They also found that AFNI had two further problems. It underestimated smoothness at the group level, and its 3dClustSim tool contained a bug that shrank the volume searched for clusters, which weakened the correction for multiple comparisons.

The results they found put into question the validity of published fMRI studies that relied on parametric cluster inference, which the authors estimate at several thousand papers. They argue that researchers should share statistical maps and raw data so that past results can be rethresholded or reanalyzed (as they found that many papers were not already doing this). The broader point was that statistical methods used in this space should be validated on real data. Simulations cannot reproduce the spatial correlation structure of actual scans, which is exactly where the parametric assumptions break down.

Brown and Behrmann gives credit for the first large-scale validation of fMRI statistics on real data, but criticize the significance statement’s claim that the findings question thousands of fMRI studies. They argue the overstatement and the resulting media coverage led to unneccessary doubt with fMRI. They call for interdisciplinary work on analysis methods that model the physics and physiology of the scan rather than better significance tests alone.

The actual reframing seems right to me. If the correlation structure of real fMRI data does not match what the current test assumes, tuning another to that test is likely wrong. The harder problem is building a model of the data that reflects how the scanner and the brain actually produce the signal, and both Brown and Behrmann are arguing the field should invested more in the former than the latter. The weakness of their argument though is that it defers the actual problem. Researchers analyzing data today need a procedure or solution that works now, and "build better forward models" is a research project, not a usable recommendation. It gives no guidance to someone analyzing a dataset this month.

The creaters of AFNI repeated the simulations and confirmed that heavy tails in the spatial autocorrelation function inflate false positives, but they also showed the 3dClustSim bug changed error rates by at most 3 to 5% and that AFNI, FSL, and SPM performed similarly. They object to the headline 70% figure as an outlier rather than a typical value, putting the usual range at 20–40% at P value of 0.01 and 5–15% at P value of 0.001, and report that adding nonparametric permutation to AFNI’s 3dttest++ brought all error rates within the nominal interval.

Kessler and others reanalyzed the same task data using the false discovery rate rather than the familywise error rate, on the grounds that researchers care more about the proportion of reported clusters that are false. They found that nearly all clusters significant at CDT 0.001 survive a nonparametric FDR benchmark, while at CDT 0.01 only clusters with corrected P values below 0.00001 hold up. They also asked how readers should actually judge already-published results. 

This is the most useful of the responses, since the original paper shows that a problem exists without telling a reader how to judge any specific published result. Acting on it otherwise requires reading the methods debate closely enough to evaluate each paper yourself, which few people will do unless they work in that area. The problem is worse for the people running the software. Most users I assume are neuroscientists, not statisticians, and they rely on defaults they did not choose and cannot easily evaluate. They often learn something was wrong only when a compuatational focused lab member or collaborator tells them, which means the people producing the results can often be the least equipped to judge them upfront.
