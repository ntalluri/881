The article "Let's Practice What We Preach: Turning Tables into Graphs" gives recommendations for how to construct plots that present results in statistical research. While tables may be more beginner friendly to read and interpret, they can obscure comparisions that could be more clearly displayed and explained in a graph. The paper has specific advice depending on the data; however, in general some starting rules are keeping plots at 2D plots, putting descriptive variables on the x-axis and the outcome on the y-axis, and splitting information across several plots rather than cramming everything into one. Overall, the article shows that buidling a good graph takes work and that work correpsonds clarifying any comparisons an author considers interesting and the initial investment in turning a table into a graph leads to conclusions and comparisons that the table leaves hidden.

My first reaction to this paper was that it was useless, and a second later, my reaction was that I am actually bad at making graphs. I think I had been treating graphs as something easy to produce and as carrying less weight than the rest of a paper, when in reality making a good plot is actually really hard. Something that stuck with me while reading through this was that making graph can feel like a science itself. 

I feel like for me to make a good plot I would need a set of rules to follow, however the reality might be actually knowing what I want to plot. Thinking through it, turning a table into a graph seems to have two stages. The first is making a bad graph to find what is in the table, the second is making that trend easy to see; I think I end up staying in the first stage. What I need is less a set of rules to follow and more a clear sense of what I am trying to show, since the second stage only gets easier once I know that. I definitly have a preference on how I like to display my data as well. When I read an article and see a type of figure I do not like, I skip it until the text explains it, and when I see one I do like, I work through it on my own. That may be my own preference, it may be that I understand some plot types better than others, or it may also be that a plot I find complicated is one that could have been actually presented better.

The other thing I've always noticed is that data displays are hard to interpret without text paired with them, which seems like a flaw if the display is supposed to be the visual explanation of the trend. Reading this article, the table sitting next to the plot somehow filled that role. It gave me enough context to see what the author was pointing at in the text without reading the graph description. I had never thought to show a table and a graph together; I usually show the graph alone.
