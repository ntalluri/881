This set of papers focuses on what data science is. The main author traces how data science came to be, and the other papers respond by rejecting, acknowledging, or extending the ideas.

Based on what I read, data science is a combination of statistics, coding, machine learning, scaling to big data, and computational power. Statistics sits at the center of the field, but that part gets hidden when people describe the major, so the statistical content gets lost. Data science is also more than statistics; it is the science of learning from data. Data science can be broken down into six divisions: data gathering, preparation, and exploration, data representation and transformation, computing with data, data visualization and presentation, data modeling, and science about data science.

One point that stuck with me is that calls to reform statistics went unanswered for decades, and what finally changed the field was code. What made data analysis into something really concreate was programming langauges and libraries. Data analysis could be written as a script and became something we could download, run, modify, and measure. Swapping one estimator for another and recording whether the result improved turned data analysis into something that could be tested. The tooling seems to be what made the data science part possible in the first place.

Applied statistics can look like data science, but one of the papers drew a distinction in how each is taught. Statistics courses often hand students precooked data and walk them to the answer, while data science courses send students into the jungle to work on problems no one has solved yet. Seeing this argument makes me think that what happened is that the applied statistics major needed to change but instead a whole new field was formed to be able to do what applied statistics couldn't.

In one of the responses, the author made it seem like data science was a rebrand of what statistics is and was. Now that I am in a data science program, I still had no idea what data science really was going in. I never understood why someone would pick a data science major when a double major in computer science and statistics covers the same ground in more depth. Many of the skills from my computer science major overlap with what a data science major does, and I could go further on the computing side. What the data science major gave me in statistics, I could have gotten by taking statistics courses directly. So I understand what the major is, but I still do not see the point of it, even though it fills a gap between the two fields that did not exist before and pushes two majors to intertwine.
